Understanding Fiduciary Duty in Financial Planning

Alexandra M. Rourke, Esq.

Managing Partner, Fin Astra

Fiduciary duty is not a phrase most financial advisors lead with — and for good reason. It commits them to something most are unwilling to commit to: legally enforceable loyalty to a client's interests, above their own.

At Fin Astra, fiduciary duty is not a marketing claim. It is a legal standard that our licensed attorneys have operated under for their entire professional lives. Understanding what it actually means — and why it matters enormously in financial planning — is the purpose of this article.

What Is Fiduciary Duty?

A fiduciary is a person who holds a position of trust with respect to another person's property or interests. Fiduciary duty is the legal obligation arising from that relationship — requiring the fiduciary to act in the best interests of the beneficiary, not their own.

In its simplest form, fiduciary duty requires three things:

  • Loyalty: acting solely in the client's interest, avoiding conflicts of interest
  • Care: applying the skill and diligence of a competent professional
  • Disclosure: revealing any conflicts of interest that cannot be avoided

These obligations are familiar territory for attorneys. Lawyers have operated under fiduciary standards as a matter of professional ethics — enforced by bar licensing bodies — for as long as the legal profession has existed.

The Suitability Standard vs. The Fiduciary Standard

The key distinction in financial services is between the suitability standard and the fiduciary standard.

Under the suitability standard, a financial advisor must recommend products that are "suitable" for a client — meaning they are not obviously inappropriate. This is a low bar. A commission-generating product can be "suitable" even if a lower-cost, higher-quality alternative exists.

"Suitable is not the same as best. It is the difference between an advisor who works for you and one who works near you."

The fiduciary standard is more demanding. It requires advisors to act in the client's best interest — period. This means:

  • Recommending the lowest-cost appropriate investment, not the highest-commission one
  • Disclosing conflicts of interest before they influence advice
  • Never placing personal financial interests above the client's
  • Maintaining competence sufficient to discharge the duty of care

Why Lawyer-Led Advisory Is Different

Licensed attorneys are bound by fiduciary obligations in their professional role — through bar rules, professional responsibility codes, and common law — regardless of whether they also hold financial designations. This means the consequences for breaching fiduciary duty are not merely civil: bar admission can be revoked, criminal liability may arise, and professional indemnity claims are possible.

This is categorically different from the accountability structure of a typical registered investment adviser, where the practical consequences of breaching fiduciary duty are primarily regulatory and civil.

Practical Implications for Your Financial Planning

When you engage an advisor subject to genuine fiduciary obligations, several things change:

  • Product recommendations are evaluated on merit, not margin
  • Conflicts of interest are disclosed in writing before they influence advice
  • Fee structures are transparent and not contingent on third-party product sales
  • Documentation standards are higher — because professional liability demands it

At Fin Astra, every engagement begins with a written statement of scope, fee structure, and any relevant conflicts — none of which we are aware of in a standard engagement, because we do not accept commissions or referral fees from product providers.

How to Verify Fiduciary Status

Not every advisor who uses the word "fiduciary" is bound by it in the way that matters. Here are the questions worth asking any prospective advisor:

  • Are you a licensed attorney or a Registered Investment Adviser (RIA)?
  • Do you accept commissions or referral fees from any product provider?
  • Will you confirm in writing that you are acting as my fiduciary for this engagement?
  • Are there any conflicts of interest that apply to our relationship?

If an advisor cannot answer these questions directly and in writing, that is itself informative.
